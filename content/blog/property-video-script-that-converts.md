# How to Write a Property Video Script That Converts Viewers Into Viewings

The narration on a property video does more work than it gets credit for. Good visuals get attention; the script is what turns that attention into a booked viewing. Most narration defaults to generic filler — here's how to write (or brief an AI tool to generate) something that actually persuades.

## The problem with most property narration

"Welcome to this beautiful three-bedroom home, featuring a spacious living room and a modern kitchen" — this sentence could describe almost any listing on the market. It's not wrong, exactly, but it does no persuasive work, because it contains no information a buyer couldn't have guessed before watching.

The fix isn't more adjectives. It's more specifics.

## Specific beats superlative, every time

Compare these two lines for the same living room:

> Generic: "A beautiful, spacious living room, perfect for entertaining."

> Specific: "A south-facing living room with original 1930s parquet flooring, getting direct sun through most of the afternoon."

The second version contains facts a buyer can actually use to imagine living there — light direction, a specific material, a concrete time of day. It's also more credible, because specific claims read as honest in a way generic superlatives don't.

## A structure that works for almost any property

**Opening (first 10–15 seconds): set context, not just location.**
Don't just state the address — give a sense of the setting. "A quiet residential street a five-minute walk from [local landmark]" does more than "located on Maple Grove."

**Middle (room by room): lead with function, follow with feature.**
For each room, state what it's for and then the one detail worth remembering. "The kitchen — recently renovated, with an island that seats four" gives a buyer both the practical fact and the standout detail in one sentence.

**Transitions: use them to imply flow, not just mark a cut.**
"Through to the dining area" or "up to the first floor" does more work than silence between rooms — it reinforces the sense that a buyer is moving through a real space, not watching a slideshow.

**Closing (last 10–15 seconds): end on the strongest single reason to view in person.**
Whatever the property's single best feature is — a garden, a view, a genuinely unusual room — the narration should close on it, not on whichever room happened to be photographed last.

## Specifics worth including if you have them

- **Light and aspect** — which direction rooms face, and what that means practically (morning sun in the kitchen, evening light in the garden).
- **Materials and age** — original features, recent renovations, specific finishes. "Original" and "recently renovated" are both selling points, just to different buyers.
- **Proximity, in real terms** — "a five-minute walk" lands better than "close to" or "nearby."
- **Anything genuinely unusual** — a home office, a workshop, an unusually large garden for the area. Buyers remember the unusual detail long after they've forgotten the generic ones.

## What to leave out

- **Superlatives with nothing behind them** — "stunning," "amazing," "must-see" used without a specific reason attached read as filler and buyers have learned to discount them.
- **Anything you can't back up at viewing.** Narration that oversells creates a gap between expectation and reality that costs trust at the actual viewing.
- **Every single room, in exhaustive detail.** Not every bedroom needs its own paragraph — the second and third bedrooms can often be covered together unless one has a specific standout feature.

## Frequently asked questions

**Should narration match the tone of the property, or stay neutral?**
It's worth matching tone loosely to property type — a warmer, more conversational tone for a family home; a crisper, more efficient tone for a city apartment aimed at professionals.

**How much narration is too much?**
If the narration is talking constantly with no pauses, it starts to feel like being read a brochure rather than shown a home. Let some shots breathe without narration, particularly on a striking visual.

**Does narration need to mention the price?**
Not always — some agents prefer to let the listing's price field do that job and keep narration focused on the property itself, particularly for videos that will be shared on social media where the specific listing context isn't always visible.

## The bottom line

The difference between forgettable and persuasive property narration is almost entirely specificity. Generic language describes a category of property; specific details describe *this* property, and specific details are what a buyer actually remembers when deciding which viewing to book.
