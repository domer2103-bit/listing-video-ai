# 7 Mistakes Estate Agents Make With Listing Photos (and How Video Fixes Them)

Photos are still the foundation of any listing, but they're also where most of the avoidable mistakes happen. Here are the seven that show up most often — and why pairing photos with a narrated video walkthrough softens the damage from most of them.

## 1. No sense of flow between rooms

A gallery of individual room photos, viewed in whatever order the portal displays them, tells a buyer what each room looks like but not how they connect. Is the kitchen next to the living room, or down a hallway? Does the primary bedroom get morning or evening light relative to the kitchen's aspect? Photos alone leave this to guesswork.

**Why video fixes it:** A walkthrough sequences rooms in a logical path, giving buyers the spatial understanding that a photo grid can't.

## 2. Inconsistent lighting across the set

One agent shoots the exterior at midday and the interior at dusk with lamps on; the result is a set of photos that all look like they're from different properties, or different times of year. It reads as unpolished even when each individual photo is fine.

**Why video fixes it:** It doesn't, fully — but a good video tool can grade and pace the sequence so the inconsistency is less jarring than it is scrolling through a static gallery, and narration bridges the gap by describing consistent features regardless of how the lighting looks shot to shot.

## 3. Leading with the least interesting room

The first photo in a listing does the most work — it's what shows up in search results and thumbnail previews. Too many listings lead with an exterior shot of a fairly ordinary house front, saving the actual selling points (the renovated kitchen, the garden, the view) for photo six or seven, by which point a scrolling buyer has already moved on.

**Why video fixes it:** A video's opening seconds can be edited or generated to lead with the strongest feature, not simply the first photo taken.

## 4. Wide-angle distortion that misrepresents room size

Ultra-wide lenses make small rooms look larger in photos — until a buyer walks in and feels misled. This doesn't just cost a viewing; it costs trust, and can sour a negotiation before it starts.

**Why video fixes it:** Narration can add honest context ("a compact but well-proportioned second bedroom") that a photo alone can't, setting expectations more accurately before a viewing.

## 5. Missing the "why here" context

Photos show the property. They rarely show the setting — the street, the proximity to a park or station, what the neighbourhood actually feels like. For buyers relocating from outside the immediate area, this is often the deciding factor, and it's almost always absent from a standard photo set.

**Why video fixes it:** An establishing shot (some tools generate an AI aerial or satellite view) gives a sense of location and context before the interior tour even begins.

## 6. No emotional throughline

A photo gallery is a set of disconnected facts. It doesn't build toward anything. Buyers respond to narrative — the sense of arriving, moving through, and landing somewhere that feels like it could be home.

**Why video fixes it:** A narrated walkthrough has a beginning, middle, and end almost by construction — it opens on arrival, moves through the living spaces, and closes on the feature most likely to close the deal.

## 7. Too few photos, or too many

Both extremes hurt. Five photos looks like there's something being hidden; thirty photos of a modest three-bed house looks padded and buries the good shots among filler. The sweet spot for most residential listings is somewhere between 10 and 20 photos, prioritising quality and variety over volume.

**Why video fixes it:** A two-minute video can cover the same ground as twenty photos in a format that holds attention for the full duration, rather than relying on a buyer to click through every thumbnail.

## Fixing the photos themselves

Video helps cover for photo weaknesses, but it's not a substitute for getting the photos right in the first place. The highest-performing listings do both:

- Shoot at the time of day that suits the property's aspect (usually mid-morning for east-facing rooms, late afternoon for west-facing).
- Declutter before shooting — buyers imagining their own life in a space are distracted by someone else's.
- Get the exterior shot right, since it's almost always the first thing a buyer sees.
- Include at least one photo that shows scale accurately — a doorway or hallway in frame helps buyers judge room size honestly.

## Frequently asked questions

**How many listing photos is too many?**
Past about 25 for a typical residential property, additional photos usually dilute rather than add — buyers stop clicking through before they reach the end.

**Does adding video mean I can use fewer photos?**
Not really — most buyers still expect a full photo gallery alongside video. Video adds to the experience; it doesn't replace the expectation of thorough photos.

**Can a video fix genuinely bad photos?**
Narration and pacing can add context and polish, but there's no substitute for well-lit, well-composed source photos. If the photos are the core problem, reshoot before adding video.
